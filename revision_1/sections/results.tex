Four observations stand out. First, uncertainty is expensive: with perfect demand information the same lookahead costs \costPIBaseFresh{}, a little more than half of the best implementable policy, almost entirely because lost sales and holding vanish. Second, point forecasts are a poor input for perishable replenishment. LA-Mean plans to sell exactly the expected demand and loses \lostLAMeanBaseFresh{} per episode in lost sales (fill rate \fillLAMeanBaseFresh\%), as Proposition~\ref{prop:marginal} suggests when $p_u$ is four times $p_s$. Third, among the policies without learning, the three stock-age-based inventory rules are the cheapest: UL$_\lambda$, BSP and BSP-EW cost between \costULBaseFresh{} and \costBSPEWBaseFresh{}, with fill rates of \fillBSPBaseFresh--\fillULBaseFresh\% and waste rates of 2.3--2.6\%. With daily review, little of the current stock is at risk of expiring before it is sold, so the waste correction of BSP-EW rarely changes an order.

Fourth, integration with routing does reduce travel but loses more on the inventory side. The uncertainty-aware lookahead policies visit 7.8--9.0 stores per day instead of 8.8--9.6 and save about 460--910 in routing, but they miss the balance between waste and service that the rules strike, in one of two directions. LA-Quantile holds 31--37\% more stock than the rules and wastes twice as much (waste rate \wasteLAQBaseFresh\%): a deterministic plan that covers two or three days with quantile targets stacks a safety buffer on every covered day, even though the next day's re-planning will correct any shortfall. LA-Quantile2, which keeps the 0.9 quantile only for the implemented day and plans later days at the 0.8 quantile, halves the waste rate of LA-Quantile (\wasteLAQTwoBaseFresh\% vs.\ \wasteLAQBaseFresh\%) and costs 5.6\% less. LA-Robust and ScenPO-SS err in the other direction: they waste little (\wasteLARobBaseFresh\% and \wasteScenPOBaseFresh\%) but serve only \fillLARobBaseFresh\% and \fillScenPOBaseFresh\% of demand. The scenario set of ScenPO-SS contains no demand above the predicted 0.9 quantile of any store-day, so its single plan protects against less demand than the asymmetry between lost sales and waste warrants.

\subsection{What the decision-focused corrections learn}\label{sec:dflresults}

Table~\ref{tab:ulcorrections} summarises the learned corrections of UL-DFL on the delivery decisions it takes in the 100 earlier test episodes of the base case (the rule triggers a delivery on 90\% of the store-days; on the others the correction has no effect). On average the network lowers the order-up-to level slightly, from the tuned 0.96 quantile to an implied service level of 0.94. More important is \emph{when} it deviates, along two dimensions that a one-day order-up-to rule cannot see. On promotion days it lowers the level to an implied service level of about 0.89, i.e.\ by about a third of a day of mean demand. Promotions raise the forecast spread, so a high quantile lies far above the expected demand, and whatever is left when the promotion ends meets regular demand and ages. Over the week it raises the level on Thursday (implied level 0.97), keeps it near the tuned level on Wednesday and Friday, and lowers it from Saturday to Monday (0.91--0.92): a unit left over before the weekend is sold in the Saturday peak, whereas a unit left over after it meets lower demand for the rest of its shelf life. The age of the stock on hand matters little, because the rule's order quantity already nets out the stock projected to expire; the level is only slightly lower when units expire on the day (0.933 vs.\ 0.942). Both main corrections reduce the stock that is still on the shelf when demand falls, which is where the waste of the rule arises.

Table~\ref{tab:corrections} reports the corrections of LA-DFL on the decision states of 20 earlier test episodes. Here the dominant pattern is temporal within the plan. For the implemented day the network keeps the target at about the 0.91 quantile, essentially the tuned LA-Quantile level, but it lowers the targets of the second and third lookahead days to about the 0.80 and 0.77 quantiles. In inventory terms, the network has learned that only today's shortfall is irreversible: tomorrow's planned demand will be re-planned with tomorrow's stock and forecast, so a full safety buffer on that day mostly turns into carried stock and, with a three-day shelf life, into waste. This is precisely the recourse that a deterministic lookahead ignores and that Proposition~\ref{prop:dfl} lets the gradient account for. Context-specific corrections are small: targets are lowered somewhat more on promotion days and slightly less when the store holds more or older stock. The day-specific levels learned by LA-DFL are those that the tuning of LA-Quantile2 selects, $(0.9,0.8)$, which explains why the two policies perform alike.

\subsection{Can re-tuning the rule reproduce UL-DFL?}\label{sec:frontier}

A natural objection is that UL-DFL merely finds better values of the rule's parameters. For the first version of this study the objection was partly right: UL$_\lambda$ had been tuned on a coarse grid, and about half of the advantage of UL-DFL over it disappeared once the rule was tuned on a fine grid (Appendix~\ref{app:variants}). We therefore tune the rule on the fine grid and train UL-DFL from it. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} places UL-DFL in the waste--service plane together with 124 parameterisations of the rules, all evaluated on the earlier base-case test episodes: UL$_\lambda$ with $\lambda=1$, ten service levels $\alpha$ between 0.85 and 0.99 and eight trigger levels $\beta$ between 0.5 and 0.99, UL$_\lambda$ with $\lambda\in\{2,3\}$, and daily BSP and BSP-EW at the same ten service levels. No parameterisation serves at least UL-DFL's fill rate (96.3\%) with no more waste: at that fill rate the least waste any rule achieves is 2.49\% of the delivered units, against 2.09\% for UL-DFL. The learned corrections move the policy beyond the waste--service frontier of the rules, which no choice of a single service level and trigger can do.

The comparison in total cost favours the rules, because the cheapest of the 124 rules is selected on the test episodes themselves, an advantage UL-DFL does not have. This rule, UL$_\lambda$ with $\alpha=0.96$ and $\beta=0.8$, costs 10,715 per episode; UL-DFL is still 0.6\% cheaper (95\% CI $[-113, -17]$) and wastes 25\% less (2.09\% vs.\ 2.80\% of the delivered units). The real-data case gives a different picture: there the rule selected on validation episodes ($\alpha=0.96$, $\beta=0.85$) is also the cheapest rule in hindsight, and five parameterisations serve at least UL-DFL's fill rate with no more waste. On these data the learned corrections do not move the policy beyond the frontier of the rules (Section~\ref{sec:realresults}).

\subsection{Does the learner need the rule?}\label{sec:nndfl}

The converse question is whether the rule contributes anything once a network is trained on realised cost. NN-DFL answers it: it has the same inputs, differentiable simulation, loss, seeds and model selection as UL-DFL, but outputs the delivery directly, without the visit trigger and order-up-to structure of UL$_\lambda$. On the fresh base-case test episodes it costs \costNNDFLBaseFresh{} per episode, \ULabsrelNNDFLBaseFresh\% more than UL-DFL (95\% CI of UL-DFL minus NN-DFL \ULciNNDFLBaseFresh{}) and more than the tuned UL$_\lambda$ it would replace. It does learn to waste little (waste rate \wasteNNDFLBaseFresh\%), but it loses more sales and, lacking a trigger, visits more stores (\visitsNNDFLBaseFresh{} per day against \visitsULDFLBaseFresh{}). Across the thirteen configurations UL-DFL is significantly cheaper than NN-DFL in twelve, by 0.6--5.1\% (Table~\ref{tab:uldfldiffs}), and not significantly different on the real data. The gap is largest with expensive routing, where the trigger of UL-DFL cuts store visits to \visitsULDFLRouteThree{} per day while NN-DFL keeps visiting \visitsNNDFLRouteThree{}. NN-DFL is never significantly cheaper than UL$_\lambda$ itself and significantly more expensive in nine configurations. These comparisons are secondary, because the ablation was added after the primary hypotheses were fixed, but they are consistent: with the same network and learning signal, the gain comes from placing the learner on the rule.

\subsection{Real-data case}\label{sec:realresults}

On observed demand (column ``Real data'' of Tables~\ref{tab:sensitivity} and~\ref{tab:uldfldiffs}) the ranking of the policy families is the same as in the synthetic base case, but decision-focused learning of the order-up-to level does not pay. The re-tuned UL$_\lambda$ is the cheapest policy (\costULFresh{} per episode), and UL-DFL is \ULabsrelULFresh\% more expensive (95\% CI \ULciULFresh{}). Without learning, the rules (\costULFresh--\costBSPEWFresh) are again cheaper than every integrated policy, the best of which, LA-Quantile2, costs \costLAQTwoFresh{}, and LA-Mean is again the most expensive. Service is lower than in the synthetic study for all policies (fill rate \fillULFresh\% for UL$_\lambda$), because observed demand is more volatile and its promotion and weekend peaks occasionally exceed the daily fleet capacity.

The failure of UL-DFL is one of transfer, not of training. On the validation episodes all three networks improved on the rule, by 0.6--0.9\%, and model selection therefore kept them; on the test episodes all three are more expensive than the rule (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}). Validation and test episodes come from different products (28 and 56 products), and the corrections the networks learn are tied to the demand patterns of the 101 training products. They are more than twice as large as in the synthetic base case (standard deviation of $\delta_\theta$ 0.73 against 0.30) and follow a pronounced weekly pattern: the order-up-to level is raised on Friday (implied service level 0.97) and lowered from Saturday to Monday (0.87, 0.80 and 0.87). For the test products this removes some waste (waste rate \wasteULDFLFresh\% against \wasteULFresh\%) but costs more in lost sales (fill rate \fillULDFLFresh\% against \fillULFresh\%). Validation-based model selection protects against harmful corrections only when the validation episodes are representative of those on which the policy is used; with few products and strong product-specific patterns, a larger validation set or a stronger penalty on deviations from the rule would be needed. LA-DFL is \absrelLAQFresh\% cheaper than LA-Quantile, which is not significant (Holm-adjusted $p=\pholmLAQFresh$), and LA-Quantile2 is again cheaper than LA-DFL, though not significantly.

\subsection{Store-level service}\label{sec:stores}

Average fill rates hide how service is distributed over the network (Figure~\ref{fig:stores}). In all three layouts the inventory rules, with or without learning, serve every store at least 95.4\%. Their orders do not depend on where a store is located, and neither does their service. The deterministic lookahead policies (LA-Quantile, LA-Quantile2, LA-Robust, LA-DFL) do not serve the stores alike: their worst-served store reaches only 89.1--90.3\% in the base layout (fresh test episodes) and 89.6--94.5\% in the clustered and mixed layouts. What matters is not the distance from the depot but the travel a visit adds relative to what the store sells. We measure it by the detour of inserting the store into a tour through all stores, divided by its mean daily demand. In the base and mixed layouts one store stands out on this measure (1.3 distance units per unit of demand, against at most 0.8 for every other store), and in both layouts it is the worst-served store of every deterministic lookahead policy (89.1--93.6\%), whereas the rules, with or without learning, serve it at 95.8--96.8\%. Consolidating deliveries saves the most travel at such a store, and the plan then accepts shortages there. In the clustered layout, where every detour is short (at most 0.3), the shortfall of the lookahead policies is smaller (worst store 92.3--94.5\%). With ten stores per layout this is an observation about individual stores rather than an estimated relationship. ScenPO-SS serves every store less well (worst store 90.9--91.9\% across the layouts), but its shortfall is not concentrated at the expensive store (92.0--92.9\% there). This effect is relevant for retailers with service-level agreements per store: an integrated plan may concentrate stock-outs at individual stores even when its average fill rate is high.

\subsection{Computation}\label{sec:computation}

All \ndecisionsBaseFresh{} lookahead decisions on the fresh base-case test episodes were solved to a proven gap of at most \maxgapBaseFresh\%, with \nonoptBaseFresh{} time-limit stops and \fallbacksBaseFresh{} fallbacks; the same holds in every configuration reported. (LA-Quantile was evaluated a second time on these episodes because the computer entered standby during its first run, which stopped decisions at the wall-clock time limit; the policy is deterministic and nothing else changed.) With 12 decisions solved in parallel, a deterministic lookahead decision takes about \secsLAQBaseFresh{} seconds and a ScenPO-SS decision with 20 scenarios \secsScenPOBaseFresh{} seconds; the rules, with or without learned corrections, need milliseconds. Training one UL-DFL network (30 epochs over 200 episodes, including closed-loop validation after every epoch) took about two minutes; training one LA-DFL network (2,100 states, eight epochs including MILP re-solves and closed-loop validation) about 45 minutes. The complete base case, including tuning of all policies and three seeds of each learner, took 4.2 hours, and each other configuration 1.0--2.6 hours with 12--15 parallel workers (training three NN-DFL networks adds about ten minutes).

The lookahead policies are deterministic: solving the same decision twice returns the same plan. Their closed-loop cost is nevertheless sensitive to negligible changes in the planning input, because several plans lie within the 0.5\% optimality gap and the solver returns the first one it proves; a different plan on one day changes the stock and therefore every later decision. To quantify this noise floor we re-ran LA-Quantile on the earlier test episodes with its planning targets rounded to single precision, a relative change below $10^{-7}$. The plan changed on at least one day in each of the \noiseN{} test episodes of the thirteen configurations, and episode costs moved by \noiseSDBase\% (standard deviation) in the base case, between \noiseMinBase\% and \noiseMaxBase\%, and by \noiseSDLo--\noiseSDHi\% across configurations. Averaged over 100 episodes these changes largely cancel (mean change at most \noiseAbsMeanMax\% in absolute value, standard error at most \noiseSEHi\%). Paired comparisons remain valid, because this variation is part of the episode-level noise they account for, but differences of well below one percent between two lookahead policies should not be interpreted as a systematic effect. The rule-based policies, including UL-DFL, do not solve an optimisation model for their orders and are not affected.
