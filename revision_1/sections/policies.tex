\section{Replenishment policies}\label{sec:policies}
% ============================================================================

All policies receive the same information each day: the observed age-structured stock $B_t$ and the same contextual forecasts. They differ only in how the forecasts and the stock are turned into deliveries and routes. Table~\ref{tab:policies} summarises the twelve policies and NB-DFL, a second ablation of UL-DFL (Section~\ref{sec:uldfl}); parameters marked as tuned are selected on validation episodes only. Decision-focused learning is applied at two levels: on top of the deliver-up-to-level rule (UL-DFL, Section~\ref{sec:uldfl}), where it learns the order-up-to level, and on top of the deterministic lookahead (LA-DFL, Section~\ref{sec:dfl}), where it learns the planning targets.

\subsection{Shared forecasts}\label{sec:forecasts}

For every store $i$, planning day $t$ and lookahead offset $k\in\{0,\dots,H-1\}$, one LightGBM model \citep{ke2017lightgbm} predicts the mean $\hat\mu_{i,t+k}$ and thirteen LightGBM quantile models predict the quantiles $\hat Q_{\tau}(D_{i,t+k}\mid\xi)$ for $\tau\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2,\dots,0.9,0.95,0.975,0.99\}$ (sorted to avoid crossing). Features are store identity, offset $k$, calendar, announced discount and holiday, temperature forecast, and summaries of the last 1, 7 and 28 days of demand. A forecast scale is $\hat\sigma=(\hat Q_{0.9}-\hat Q_{0.1})/(2z_{0.9})$, and quantiles at other levels are linearly interpolated. Quantile regression of this type is the standard data-driven newsvendor input \citep{ban2019big,huber2019data}.

\subsection{Inventory-control rules with route-second delivery}\label{sec:rules}

These policies represent current replenishment practice: an inventory rule sets the store orders, and the orders are then routed. Deliveries are routed by an exact capacitated vehicle-routing oracle that enumerates all feasible route sets (possible because $n=10$). If the fleet cannot carry all orders, the part exceeding today's median demand is reduced proportionally until a feasible packing exists.

\paragraph{BSP: periodic base-stock.} Stores are served on a fixed cycle of $R\in\{1,2\}$ days (for $R=2$ in two alternating groups). On a delivery day, store $i$ is raised to the order-up-to level $S_{it}=\hat Q_\alpha\big(\sum_{k<R}D_{i,t+k}\big)$ (normal approximation for $R>1$). The inventory position counts all units regardless of age, as in most retail ordering systems \citep{haijema2019improved}.

\paragraph{BSP-EW: base-stock with estimated waste.} Identical to BSP, except that units of the current stock that are expected to expire unsold during the cycle are removed from the inventory position: $q_{it}=(S_{it}-\sum_r B_{irt}+\widehat{EW}_{it})^{+}$, where $\widehat{EW}_{it}$ is computed by projecting the age profile forward under FIFO with the mean forecasts. This is the ``estimated withdrawal and aging'' idea of \citet{broekmeulen2009heuristic}; waste-anticipating base-stock rules of this family were found to be near-optimal among practical stock-age-dependent policies by \citet{haijema2019improved}.

\paragraph{UL$_\lambda$: deliver-up-to-level rule \citep{crama2018stochastic}.} A store is visited when its stock does not cover the current day's demand at service level $\beta$, i.e.\ when $\sum_r B_{irt}<\hat Q_\beta(D_{it})$; it then receives enough to reach the $\alpha$-quantile of the demand of the next $\lambda$ days, capped by its shelf room. This is the state-dependent ``deliver-up-to-level'' rule that \citet{crama2018stochastic} identify as a simple policy with good performance in some settings; it acts as an $(R,s,S)$ policy with $R=1$. Our implementation differs from the original in four disclosed respects. (i) The original uses one target service level for both the trigger and the delivery quantity (90\% in its experiments); we tune the two levels separately on validation cost, and the grid contains the original's case $\beta=\alpha$. (ii) The original counts the total stock; here the order-up-to level is reached net of the stock projected to expire within the $\lambda$ days, as in BSP-EW, while the trigger counts the total stock as in the original. (iii) The quantiles come from the shared contextual forecasts instead of a known demand distribution. (iv) The original routes the selected deliveries by solving a vehicle routing problem, in its experiments with a fast LP-based heuristic; here they are routed exactly. The original compares $\lambda=1$ with $\lambda=m-1$; we tune $\lambda\in\{1,2,3\}$. Appendix~\ref{app:variants} evaluates the rule exactly as published, tuned on the same validation episodes; it is 0.6--3.4\% more expensive than our version in every configuration.

\subsection{Decision-focused order-up-to levels (UL-DFL)}\label{sec:uldfl}

UL-DFL (proposed) keeps the visit trigger and the delivery logic of UL$_\lambda$ and learns how far the order-up-to level of each store-day should deviate from the forecast quantile:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uldfl}
\tilde S_{it}(\theta)=S_{it}+\hat s_{it}\,\delta_\theta(\phi_{it}),\qquad q_{it}=\mathbf 1\Big[\textstyle\sum_r B_{irt}<\hat Q_\beta(D_{it})\Big]\,\Big(\tilde S_{it}-\textstyle\sum_r B_{irt}+\widehat{EW}_{it}\Big)^{+},
\end{equation}
where $S_{it}=\hat Q_\alpha\big(\sum_{k<\lambda}D_{i,t+k}\big)$ is the tuned rule's level, $\hat s_{it}$ the forecast scale of the $\lambda$-day demand, and $\delta_\theta$ a two-layer network (32 SiLU units per layer) whose output layer is initialised to zero, so that training starts exactly at the tuned UL$_\lambda$. The input $\phi_{it}$ contains the store's forecast features for day $t$ (discount, promotion and holiday flags, temperature forecast, weekday, recent demand), the scaled mean, scale and 5--95\% range of the forecast, and the store's stock by remaining life. A correction $\delta_\theta=0.5$ raises the level by half a forecast standard deviation; under a normal approximation it moves the implied service level from $\alpha$ to $\Phi(z_\alpha+\delta_\theta)$.

The rule covers only $\lambda$ days and ignores what happens to the stock afterwards, in particular that units delivered today are carried into tomorrow and expire after $m$ days. UL-DFL learns this from the realised cost of whole episodes. On a training episode the rule is simulated for all 21 decision days with the age-structured FIFO dynamics of Section~\ref{sec:system}, written as a differentiable program: sales, waste and carried stock are compositions of minima and differences of the stock vector and the deliveries, shelf capacity caps each delivery, and fleet capacity scales the day's deliveries down proportionally when their total exceeds $KQ$. The visit trigger is held fixed (it is piecewise constant in $\theta$). Back-propagation through time then returns the gradient of the episode's holding, waste and lost-sales cost with respect to $\theta$. Following Proposition~\ref{prop:marginal}, and with the stationary points characterised in Proposition~\ref{prop:dfl}(b), an extra unit in a delivery is pushed up when, in hindsight, it would have prevented a lost sale on any later day, and pushed down when it would have been carried for several nights or wasted; unlike the lookahead-based learner below, these effects are traced over the whole episode rather than over the $H$ days of a plan. Neural ordering policies trained through simulated inventory dynamics in this way have been proposed for single-location lost-sales systems by \citet{alvo2023neural}; we use the same principle to correct, rather than replace, a published PIRP rule.

Exact packing, routing and routing cost do not enter the gradient. They enter model selection: after every epoch the current network is run in the full closed-loop simulator on the validation episodes, and the network with the lowest validation cost, which may be the tuned rule itself, is kept. We train three networks from different random seeds and keep the one with the lowest validation cost. Once trained, UL-DFL needs one forward pass per store and day, so it is as fast online as UL$_\lambda$.

\paragraph{NN-DFL: the same learner without the rule.} To separate the contribution of the rule's structure from that of learning, NN-DFL uses the same inputs, the same differentiable episode simulation, loss, seeds and validation-based model selection, but none of the structure of UL$_\lambda$: the network outputs the delivery directly, $q_{it}=\bar d_i\,(1+\delta_\theta(\phi_{it}))^{+}$ with $\bar d_i$ the store's mean daily demand, a store is visited whenever $q_{it}>0$, and training starts from delivering one day of mean demand to every store every day. This is the type of neural ordering policy trained through simulated inventory dynamics by \citet{alvo2023neural}. Because it starts far from a good policy, it is given twice as many epochs as UL-DFL. \textbf{NB-DFL} separates the two parts of the rule that NN-DFL removes: it keeps the order-up-to structure but not the rule's forecast quantile and visit trigger. The network outputs the order-up-to level, $\tilde S_{it}=\bar d_i(1+\delta_\theta(\phi_{it}))^+$, the store receives $(\tilde S_{it}-U_{it})^+$ with $U_{it}$ its usable stock, it is visited whenever this is positive, and training starts from a level of one day of mean demand. It is trained like NN-DFL.

\subsection{Integrated rolling-horizon policies}\label{sec:la}

These policies solve the lookahead MILP of Section~\ref{sec:lookahead} every day with $H=3$ ($H=2$ when $m=2$) and implement day 0. They differ only in the planning demand.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{LA-Mean} uses the point forecasts $\hat\mu$: the classical predict-then-optimise policy.
\item \textbf{LA-Quantile} uses $\hat Q_\alpha$ for every store and day. With $\alpha$ tuned on validation episodes, this is a parametric cost-function approximation in the sense of \citet{powell2019unified}: a deterministic lookahead whose inputs are adjusted to perform well under uncertainty.
\item \textbf{LA-Quantile2} uses $\hat Q_{\alpha_0}$ on the implemented day and $\hat Q_{\alpha_1}$ on all later lookahead days, with $(\alpha_0,\alpha_1)$ tuned. It is an ablation of LA-DFL: it can represent the day-specific safety stock that LA-DFL learns (Section~\ref{sec:dflresults}) but none of its context- or stock-dependent corrections.
\item \textbf{LA-Robust} uses budgeted demand uncertainty \citep{bertsimas2006robust}. Demand on lookahead day $k$ ranges over $[\hat\mu_k,\hat\mu_k+\hat\delta_k]$ with $\hat\delta_k=\hat Q_{0.95}-\hat\mu_k$, and the cumulative scaled deviation of the first $k+1$ days is at most $\Gamma_k=\min\{\Gamma\sqrt{k+1},k+1\}$. The planning path reproduces the worst-case cumulative demand of every prefix, which is the deterministic equivalent shown by \citet{bertsimas2006robust} for inventory systems and used in robust inventory routing \citep{solyali2012robust}. $\Gamma$ is tuned.
\item \textbf{ScenPO-SS} is the Scenario Predict-then-Optimize method of \citet{jia2025scenario}, with its scenario set: the predicted deciles $0.1,\dots,0.9$ of every store-day are themselves scenarios, and eleven further scenarios each draw one band between neighbouring deciles uniformly and then every store-day value uniformly inside that band, giving 20 equally weighted scenarios as in the original study. The single-stage scenario model chooses one delivery and routing plan that minimises the average cost over the scenarios. Two adaptations are needed and disclosed: inventory follows the perishable age-structured dynamics with lost sales instead of the original non-perishable backlogging model, and the quantile model is LightGBM rather than an MQ-RNN, so that all policies use the same forecasts. Appendix~\ref{app:variants} reports a variant that samples ten scenarios from bands of the full quantile grid.
\item \textbf{LA-DFL} uses decision-focused planning targets (Section~\ref{sec:dfl}); in this study it serves mainly as a diagnostic of the deterministic lookahead.
\item \textbf{PI} solves the same lookahead with the realised demand of the next $H$ days. It is a value-of-information reference, not a lower bound.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Decision-focused planning targets (LA-DFL)}\label{sec:dfl}

LA-DFL keeps the deterministic lookahead, which is fast and easy to explain to planners, and learns how much each planning target should deviate from the forecast. Its role in this study is diagnostic: its corrections show where a deterministic plan mis-stocks (Section~\ref{sec:dflresults}), and the ablation LA-Quantile2 tests whether that diagnosis is enough. For store $i$, planning day $t$ and offset $k$ the target is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:target}
\tilde d_{i,t+k}(\theta)=\Big(\hat Q_{\alpha^\star}(D_{i,t+k}\mid\xi)+\hat\sigma_{i,t+k}\,\delta_\theta(\phi_{itk})\Big)^{+},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha^\star$ is the quantile level selected for LA-Quantile, and $\delta_\theta$ is a two-layer neural network whose output layer is initialised to zero, so that training starts exactly at the tuned LA-Quantile policy. The input $\phi_{itk}$ contains the forecast features, the scaled mean, scale and 5--95\% range of the forecast, the offset $k$, and the store's current stock by remaining life. The correction can therefore depend on context (e.g.\ promotions, which raise both mean and variance) and on stock age, which neither the forecasts nor a single quantile level capture.

\paragraph{Learning signal.} The visit pattern chosen by the MILP is piecewise constant in the targets, so its gradient carries no information. Given the pattern, however, deliveries are piecewise linear in the targets: each delivery covers the planned demand of its coverage window $W_k$, net of the usable stock projected by FIFO, capped by the remaining shelf room and scaled to the vehicle capacity. We implement this coverage map $q(\tilde d;V)$ as a differentiable layer and evaluate the realised $H$-day FIFO cost of the resulting deliveries under the observed demand with a differentiable simulator. Automatic differentiation then returns
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grad}
\nabla_\theta\, C_{\mathrm{FIFO}}\big(q(\tilde d_\theta;V),D\big)=\Big(\frac{\partial \tilde d}{\partial\theta}\Big)^{\!\top}\Big(\frac{\partial q}{\partial \tilde d}\Big)^{\!\top}\nabla_q C_{\mathrm{FIFO}},
\end{equation}
whose last factor is the marginal value of a delivery bounded in Proposition~\ref{prop:marginal}, and whose stationary points are characterised in Proposition~\ref{prop:dfl}. In words: if, in hindsight, an extra unit in a store's delivery would have prevented a lost sale, the targets of the days that delivery covered are pushed up; if the extra unit would have been wasted or carried, they are pushed down; and nothing changes when the store was not visited or capacity was binding.

\paragraph{Training.} Training states are the decision epochs visited by the tuned LA-Quantile policy on training episodes. Each epoch re-solves the MILP at the current targets to refresh the visit patterns (a form of solution caching, \citealp{mulamba2021contrastive}), takes Adam steps on the average realised cost of minibatches, and evaluates the closed-loop cost of the current network on the validation episodes; the network with the lowest validation cost is kept, which may be the LA-Quantile starting point. Algorithm~\ref{alg:dfl} summarises the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Training of decision-focused planning targets (LA-DFL)}\label{alg:dfl}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require training states $\{(B^{(s)},\xi^{(s)},D^{(s)})\}$ from LA-Quantile runs; tuned level $\alpha^\star$; epochs $E$
\State initialise $\delta_\theta\equiv 0$; $\theta^\star\gets\theta$; $J^\star\gets$ validation cost of LA-Quantile
\For{$e=1,\dots,E$}
  \State for every state $s$: solve the lookahead MILP with targets $\tilde d_\theta^{(s)}$; store visit pattern $V^{(s)}$
  \For{each minibatch $\mathcal B$}
    \State $L\gets |\mathcal B|^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal B} C_{\mathrm{FIFO}}\big(q(\tilde d^{(s)}_\theta;V^{(s)}),D^{(s)}\big)$ \Comment{differentiable coverage layer + FIFO simulator}
    \State $\theta\gets\mathrm{Adam}(\theta,\nabla_\theta L)$
  \EndFor
  \State $J\gets$ closed-loop validation cost of LA-DFL with $\theta$; \textbf{if} $J<J^\star$ \textbf{then} $\theta^\star\gets\theta$, $J^\star\gets J$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\theta^\star$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The two learners differ in what the gradient sees. LA-DFL learns from the $H$-day cost of one plan with the visit pattern chosen by the MILP, so it can only correct the plan's targets; UL-DFL learns from the cost of whole episodes, so it can trade today's order against tomorrow's carried stock and waste, but it keeps the rule's simple visit trigger. LA-DFL needs an MILP solve per training state and epoch; UL-DFL needs none. Three design choices of LA-DFL matter. The learner does not differentiate a heuristic surrogate of the MILP; the MILP is solved to a proven gap and only supplies visit patterns. The loss is the realised physical cost rather than regret against a full-information plan. And training and model selection are performed on the closed-loop system that is evaluated.
